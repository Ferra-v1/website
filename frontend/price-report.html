4.8%less each year

What used robots sell for.

Sellers ask about 4.8% less for a robot with each year of age, with a likely range of 2.9% to 6.6%. The rate compares robots of different ages listed at the same time, so it describes the market today. Among the 60 listings about 10 years old, most ask $7,700 to $28,600.

26 robot families have 8 or more prices behind them.

What a 5-year-old robot would likely fetch at auction.

The likely auction price is the asking estimate times the 25% share auctions pay.

How sure. A likely auction price misses a real sale by a median 39%: we predict each of 79 sales from the other auction days, and 29 land within 30%.

25%of asking

How far auctions land below asking.

The middle robot at auction brought 25% of what sellers ask for the same model at the same age, across 79 sales from BTM Industrial, Grays, Surplex and Troostwijk. The share is the same at every age in this data. Every matched sale is a factory arm. We call the asking estimate times that share the likely auction price, because an auction is close to what a lender gets back in a forced sale.

How sure. Predicting each sale from the other auction days misses by a median 39%, and 29 of 79 land within 30%. Trained only on sales before 1 Jan 2024 and tested on the 30 since, it misses by 35%, and 11 of 30 land within 30%.

32months

Rent against what a robot is worth.

Across 14 pairs of a monthly rent and a price for the same robot, the middle robot rents for 3.1% of its price a month, so about 32 months of rent add up to the price. Most rents are posted offers, and the count assumes the robot is rented every month.

Resale sits far lower. A factory arm at auction brings about 25% of its asking price, and a Tennant T7AMR floor scrubber whose $500-a-month autonomy subscription lapses can only be driven by hand. The money depends on the robot staying rented.

No robot type here has both a rent and an auction price for the same model, so this chapter sets rent against price and shows resale beside it.

55.2%paid with loans or leases

How robot fleets get paid for.

Of 2025 United States spending on plant, equipment and software, 55.2% was paid for with loans or leases. Every robot operator raise we found in 2026 was money from investors: Serve Robotics raised $84.9 million of $85.5 million by selling stock.

Equipment Leasing and Finance Association, industry overview, 2025 data; company filings, 2026.

How we work it out.

Each asking price is fitted to its model, its marketplace and its age, with one yearly rate of value loss across arms, cobots and palletizers. Auction sales are matched to the same model and build year, and the middle share sets the likely auction price. A seller with many robots counts as one source.

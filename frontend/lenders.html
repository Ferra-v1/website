A forced sale only covers this loan late on.

Collateral is what a lender can sell to get its money back: here, 50 robot arms that cost $6,250,000 new and installed. The advance rate is the share lent: 70%, paid back in equal monthly parts over 36 months, plus interest. The pale dots are our model's guess at value, and no real loan has tested it.

To be covered on the first day by a 24% sale, lend no more than this.

Counting everything installed: $1,500,000, against the $6,250,000 the arms, grippers, controllers and fitting cost.

Counting the arms alone: about $270,000, for 50 arms at the $22,500 sellers typically ask for one.

Our example lends 70%, so neither sum pays the lender back on the first day. Until a sale covers what is left, about month 30 or month 35, the monthly payments are what protect a lender. A real lender may lend far less.

Both sums rest on one figure, the middle of just 10 auction sales, and those sales were used arms. None was sold fully installed, so we cannot yet say which sum is closer. See every sale. How we work out the falling value.

What if a loan goes bad?

The lender can sell the robots. As the sums above show, that may not cover the loan. Our model says to plan on losing about this share of a whole loan, on average.

These move little because, in our model, about one loan in five ever reaches a sale, and on average more than half of it is paid back by then. No real loan has tested that.

Nobody has measured how often robot companies fail. We assume 9% a year for this robot, so one fails in about 23% of 36-month loans. We assume someone keeps the robots running in 55% of those failures and the payments go on for longer, so the robots stop in about 10%. If nobody ever did, the rows would read about 7%, 9% and 10%.

A sale's share is of what everything installed is worth after a failure cuts it: to 45% if the robots stop, 70% if they keep running. 13 of the 18 key inputs are our own assumptions. What we do not know yet →

FerraExample terms

Equipment loan, robot fleet

Collateral
50 industrial arms, each a complete palletizing cell, installed. The lender can sell these if the Loan is not repaid.
Fleet Value
$6,250,000, the price of each robot on the Price List, added up.
Advance Rate
70% of Fleet Value. This is the share of the value that gets lent.
Loan
$4,375,000, the Fleet Value times the Advance Rate.
Repayment
Equal monthly amounts of principal over 36 months from close.
Fee
About 1.5% of the Loan at close: $65,625.
Arranger
Ferra, which prices the robots and arranges the Loan.
Lender
A private credit lender that finances equipment and provides the money.

Real terms depend on the lender and the robots. This is an example. It is not an offer or a promise to lend.

Where the numbers come from

  • Our example is 50 new robot arms, each fully installed with its gripper and controller, at $125,000 each, or $6,250,000 in all. A loan of 70% is $4,375,000, with a fee of about 1.5% ($65,625) when the loan starts, paid back in equal monthly parts over 36 months. For a used robot, our example lends 70% of our estimate of what it is worth, set up and working. Real terms depend on the lender and the robots. Nothing on this site is an offer or commitment to lend.
  • From Ferra's study of 18 robot types (17 pages). Both figures are over the whole life of a loan. The 0.3% leaves out losses caused by robot companies failing; the 5.2% includes every cause. The 0.3% and 5.2% are for a new pallet-stacking robot with a mid-size customer and 70% lent.
  • 1,744 public used-robot listings from 7 marketplaces and 26 auction results from 2018 to 2026, collected for Ferra's price tool. Listings show asking prices; auction results show prices paid.

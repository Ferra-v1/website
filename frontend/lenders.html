What our study found

We modeled how 18 types of robot hold their value and what a lender would lose if a fleet had to be sold. The study runs 17 pages.

How pricing works

Asking prices on used-robot marketplaces set an upper bound, and prices paid at auction set the floor. Each robot's model, build year and condition place it against its own family and age.

Listings and auctions price the arm alone. The value of a complete installed cell comes from the risk engine's curve, an assumption we state openly.

What a lender receives

Three documents per fleet: the proposed terms, a price for every robot and the study behind the prices.

Where the numbers come from

  • Our example is 50 robot arms, each fully installed with its gripper and controller, at $125,000 each, or $6,250,000 in all. A loan of 70% is $4,375,000, with a fee of about 1.5% ($65,625) when the loan starts, paid back in equal monthly parts over 36 months. Real terms depend on the lender and the robots. Nothing on this site is an offer or commitment to lend.
  • Example values by age follow our model for a fully installed robot: about 80% of the new price after one year, 60% after two, 44% after three. Robot arms listed for sale price the arm alone, so they sit lower. See how we work it out.
  • From Ferra's study of 18 robot types (17 pages). The 0.3% leaves out losses caused by robot companies failing; the 5.2% includes every cause.
  • 1,744 public used-robot listings from 7 marketplaces and 26 auction results from 2018 to 2026, collected for Ferra's price tool. Listings show asking prices; auction results show prices paid.

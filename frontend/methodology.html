One robot, start to finish

Take a KUKA KR 210 arm built in 2020, set up and working. Here are six prices for it, drawn to the same scale.

The whole robot

  • $125,000What the whole robot cost, in our example
  • $63,300What our model says it is worth today
  • $44,300A loan at 70% of that

The arm alone

  • $16,300What sellers ask
  • $11,500What it would likely sell for
  • $8,100What auctions pay

The pale part of that bar is $32,800. On the first day, the loan is that much more than the arm alone would likely sell for.

The arm alone

What sellers ask. Asking prices for arms fall about 4.1% a year. A 6-year-old KR 210 asks about 6% less than its family as a whole. Most listings ask between $9,600 and $27,900, and 27 listings of this exact model feed the estimate.

What auctions pay. Robots under 10 years old brought 50% of the typical asking price at auction, across 6 sales. That is few, so we treat it as rough. Some of those sales are one robot type at one auction. Counted once, about 28%.

What it would likely sell for. We take a price halfway between the two, measured in percent, so each step down is the same 29%. The rule changes at 10 years, so a 10-year-old arm reads far lower than a 9-year-old one: about $5,800 against about $10,200. The drop comes from that change of rule. We use one auction figure for arms under 10 and a lower one for older arms.

The whole robot

What it cost. A working robot also has its gripper, controller and setup, so we value it from what it cost. $125,000 is our example.

What it is worth today. Our model lowers the price paid along its curve for 2 years, then at the rate real listings lose value.

Why the loan can be more than the arm alone. The loan is sized on the whole working robot and the rent it earns. In our model, a robot that is set up and renting is worth far more than the same arm on a marketplace.

That has a limit. If the robot could only be sold as the arm alone, it would bring about $11,500 and the lender would lose the rest of the $44,300. So a real lender may count less and offer less. Ferra does not lend the money, and the lender sets the real terms.

Whether the backup partner works

No customer has signed a lease that keeps paying through a backup partner if the robot company fails, and nobody has yet asked factories whether they would.

What most robots really sell for

Every listing is an asking price. Only the auction results are real sales, and there are few of them.

How prices move over time

Most prices were quoted in euros and converted at one exchange rate. Our listings are one snapshot, so we see older robots priced today, and we have not watched one robot's price fall over the years.

The price

What sellers ask, what auctions pay, and where a robot's price lands between them.

Where prices come from

Two kinds of price go in: what sellers ask and what auctions pay.

Listings. Asking prices for used robots on online marketplaces. One marketplace alone lists 28,258 used industrial robots. Nobody has paid these prices yet, and they run above what robots sell for, so we treat them as the top of the range.

Auction results. Prices paid at auction when factories or companies are sold off, from 2018 to 2026. We treat them as the bottom of the range.

The robot's details. Model, build year and condition, so each robot is priced against its own family and age.

The price tool runs on 1,744 used-robot listings and 26 auction results. Duplicates are already out of that count. 738 of the listings show a price and 1,315 show the year built.

How robots are grouped

Each robot is priced against its own kind, and a company's robots are priced one at a time.

Robots are grouped by type (for example factory arms, collaborative arms, mobile robots, pallet movers, delivery and cleaning robots), and within a type by maker and model wherever enough listings exist. Our listings cover arms only so far, in 11 families. A family is one maker's line of similar arms. Robot types with no listings yet, such as cleaning and most mobile robots, say so in the price tool and follow our model's curve only.

How good the estimate is

We tested the estimate on asking prices it had not seen, holding back one group of sellers at a time.

25%how far our estimate typically misses

About half of our estimates land within 25% of the asking price, and the rest miss by more. Using only a family's middle price would typically miss by 29%.

This test used asking prices only, because no test against real sales exists yet. It shows how close we come to what sellers ask. What buyers would pay is still untested.

The estimate fits each asking price to three things: the robot's model, its marketplace and its age, with one yearly rate of loss for all arms. A seller with many robots counts as one source, so it cannot narrow the range on its own. Models with few listings lean toward their family's level.

When an estimate rests on fewer than 8 listings, the price tool says how many and widens its range.

How fast robots lose value

Asking prices across all arms fall about 4.1% a year, and the true rate could be anywhere from 1.6% to 6.6%.

Robots 15 years old or younger fall faster, about 6.6% a year across 177 listings. Older ones fall about 2.6% across 192.

Few young arms are in the data. 14 of the 369 are under 5 years old, the average is about 16, and we take out new ones. So any price we show for a new arm is our line carried back.

The rates for separate families could not be told apart, and using them predicted worse, so we use one rate for all arms.

The loan

How a price becomes a loan, and how we test that loan.

What the curve shows

Our examples use one curve to size a loan: the share of its price when new that a fully installed robot keeps at each age.

It is our risk model's curve for a pallet-stacking robot, meaning an arm set up to stack boxes on pallets. It is an assumption, and it falls faster than the 4.1% a year we see in asking prices for the arm alone. The table below shows it in dollars for each robot type.

How values become a loan

We aim to size the loan below what the robots would resell for, so that selling them could cover the debt as they age.

What a lender can sell if a loan is not paid back is called collateral. The share of the value that gets lent is called the advance. Our example lends 70% of what new robots cost. For a used robot, 70% of our estimate of what it is worth, set up and working. The actual advance depends on the robot type, its resale history and the lender.

In the price tool, each loan starts at 70% of what the robot is worth today and comes down until rent, after running costs, covers the payment 1.25 times. The largest loan it shows is 100% of that value.

How we test a loan

Our risk engine plays out a group of 50 robots month by month, 2,000 times over.

The robot company can fail, customers can stop paying or cancel, rents and resale values move with the economy, and anything unpaid at the end is met by selling the robots. The loan is paid from that cash, with 3 months of cash held in reserve and spare cash paying the loan down.

The result is expected loss, what a lender should plan to lose on average. In our model, resale value alone almost never causes a loss, because a loan is repaid from rent unless something stops the rent. Resale decides how much is lost after that.

The sums on Lenders

The Lenders page shows one example loan. Here is the reasoning behind its figures.

Say a company wants 50 robot arms that cost $6,250,000, new and installed. It would pay the lender back in equal monthly parts over 36 months, plus interest. If it stops paying, the lender could sell the robots. That is a forced sale, and the middle auction sale we could match brought 24% of what sellers ask.

Our example lends 70%. On day one, $4,375,000 is owed. A forced sale would bring $1,500,000, or about $270,000 for the arms alone, so neither sum pays the lender back on the first day. Until a sale covers what is left, about month 30 or month 35, the monthly payments are what protect a lender. A real lender may lend far less. The value of the robots on that chart is our model's guess, and no real loan has tested it.

Both sums rest on one figure, the middle of just 10 auction sales. Everything installed means the $6,250,000 the arms, grippers, controllers and fitting cost, so a forced sale brings $1,500,000. The arms alone means 50 arms at the $22,500 our line gives for a new one, about $270,000. Only 14 of the 369 listings behind that line are under 5 years old, so it is rough. The sales were all used arms and none was sold fully installed, so we cannot yet say which sum is closer.

If the payments stop, because a customer stops paying or the robot company fails, the lender can sell the robots. The loan papers would say so from the first day. Our example terms also name the rent contracts as security, so the lender could collect the rent while the robots keep working. The lender is paid first and anything left goes to the company. If the sale does not cover the loan, the lender takes the loss.

At month 18, $2,187,500 is still owed. If nothing had gone wrong the robots would be worth about $4.35 million in our model. A robot company failure cuts that to about $1.95 million if the robots stop, or about $3.05 million if someone keeps them running. All of this counts only what the robots sell for, before the costs of selling.

The middle of the 10 sales we could match to listings paid 24% of what sellers ask for the same robot at the same age. With one repeated robot counted once, about 20%. Nine of the ten are Fanuc arms, from two auction houses. The asking prices are from 2026, and these sales ran from 2019 to 2024 with no adjustment for inflation, so older sales read low.

The loss table on Lenders moves little because, in our model, about one loan in five ever reaches a sale, and on average more than half of it is paid back by then. No real loan has tested that.

Nobody has measured how often robot companies fail. We assume 9% a year for a pallet-stacking robot, so one fails in about 23% of 36-month loans. We assume someone keeps the robots running in 55% of those failures and the payments go on for longer, so the robots stop in about 10%. If nobody ever did, the rows would read about 7%, 9% and 10%.

That table is harsher than the chart above it. A failure cuts the robots' value first, to 45% if they stop and 70% if they keep running, and the sale's share comes off what is left. So on the first day a 24% sale means about $680,000 in the table if the robots stop, against $1,500,000 in the chart. 13 of the 18 key inputs are our own assumptions.

The study

Our study covered 18 robot types and runs to 17 pages.

In it, expected loss was 0.3% once robot company failure was removed, and 5.2% with all causes included. Both assume a forced sale brings 45% of the value of everything installed. The price tool runs the same engine live, with 2,000 simulated runs and the same random draws every time, so its figure for a new pallet-stacking robot reads a little differently. A shared link shows the same numbers, and changing an input shows only that input's effect. Lenders can ask us for the study summary through the lender form.

Words we use

Listing
A robot someone has offered for sale online. The price on it is what the seller asks.
Auction result
What a robot actually sold for at auction. These are the only real sales in our data.
Family
One maker's line of similar arms, such as the KUKA KR.
Fleet
A company's group of robots.
Cell
A robot arm set up for one job, with its gripper, controller and installation.
Collateral
What a lender can sell if a loan is not paid back.
Advance
The share of the robots' value that gets lent.
Expected loss
What a lender should plan to lose on average, as a share of the loan.
Operator failure
The robot company that runs the robots goes out of business.
Backup partner
A company signed up ahead of time to keep the robots running if the robot company fails. No customer has signed such a lease yet.

How fast do industrial robots lose value?

A used industrial robot arm loses about 4.1% of its asking price each year: a typical arm asks $18,200 at 5 years old, $14,700 at 10 and $9,700 at 20.

What an arm actually sells for falls faster, because buyers pay a smaller share of the asking price once it passes 10.

A loss of 4.1% a year is slow. At that rate an arm keeps about 66% of its asking price over ten years. Robot arms are built to run for many years, the big makers keep supporting old models, and rebuilt arms come back onto the market, so asking prices hold up well.

The rate is an estimate, and it has a range. Our 95% range runs from 1.6% to 6.6% a year. We checked it several ways:

  • For arms 15 years old or younger, asking prices fall faster, about 6.6% a year.
  • For arms older than 15, the fall slows to about 2.6% a year. By then an arm is priced mostly on whether it works.
  • Leaving out refurbished arms, the rate is about 2.9% a year. Sellers who rebuild old arms ask more for them, which flattens the curve at older ages.

Why sale prices fall faster than asking prices

An asking price is a hope. To see what buyers pay, we matched auction results to listings of the same robot at the same age. Arms under 10 years old brought a middle 50% of the asking price at auction. Arms 10 years or older brought a middle 17%. We call that the auction floor, and we put the expected sale of a planned private sale halfway between the floor and the asking price on a ratio scale.

That is why the lighter line on the chart steps down at 10. A typical arm would likely sell for $10,900 at 9 years old and $6,100 at 10. If you plan to sell an arm, selling before its tenth birthday is worth real money, and if you plan to buy one, an arm just past 10 is where asking prices and likely sale prices sit furthest apart.

A working robot cell loses value faster

Everything above is for a bare arm. A robot that is set up and working in a cell, with its gripper, controller, safety fencing and programming, loses value much faster, because most of that set-up work cannot be moved to another factory. Over the first years the cell's value runs far below the cost of building it, and later it settles near what the arm alone would bring on the used market, because the arm is the part that keeps its value.

Depreciation on paper is a different thing

An accountant's depreciation schedule writes a robot's cost off over a set number of years for tax and the books. It says nothing about what the robot would sell for. If you need a market value, for a sale, a loan, insurance or a dispute, use prices like the ones on this page, for the exact model and build year.

How we measured it

We fitted one model across every used arm listing with a price and a build year: the log of the asking price against the arm's age, with an adjustment for each model and each marketplace, and errors grouped by seller so one big dealer cannot dominate. The steps are on how we price robots.

Questions people ask

How much value does an industrial robot lose per year?

About 4.1% of its asking price a year for a used arm, faster when it is young (6.6% a year up to 15 years old) and slower after.

What is a 10 year old robot arm worth?

A typical used arm asks $14,700 at 10 years old, and most ask between $7,800 and $27,800. In a planned private sale it would likely bring about $6,100.

Do robots lose value faster than other factory equipment?

The bare arm holds its asking price well. A whole working cell loses value much faster, because the set-up work cannot be moved to another factory.

Why do old robots sell for so little at auction?

Buyers at auction paid a middle 17% of the asking price for arms 10 years or older. Parts, support and controller age all weigh more on an older arm.

How long do industrial robots last?

Many run well past 20 years with maintenance, and arms that old still list for sale. Their price then depends mostly on whether they work.

A company asks.

Say a company rents robots out to factories. It wants to buy 50 robot arms, fully installed, at $125,000 each. The big robot stands for all 50.

It tells us how many robots it wants and when it needs the money.

We price every robot.

We check what sellers ask for robots like these on 7 marketplaces, and what auctions have really paid.

Then we put a price on each robot, one at a time. These 50 are new, so their price is what they cost: $6,250,000 in all.

We work out the loan.

A lender wants the loan covered by what the robots could be sold for. On the first day, a rushed sale would not cover this one, and we show the sums below.

In our example the loan is 70% of what new robots cost: $4,375,000. The company pays the other 30%, $1,875,000, itself. The dark part of the robot is what the company has paid.

We find a lender.

We write it all down: the terms, a price for every robot, and the study behind our prices.

We take those papers to lenders, the investment funds that lend money for machines. Each one reads them, asks questions, and says yes or no.

Ferra is new, so this step has not happened yet.

The loan starts.

The lender and the company sign. The lender pays $4,375,000, the robots are bought, and they go to work.

The company pays Ferra's fee, once, on this day: about 1.5% of the loan, or $65,625.

Every month, the company pays.

The robots earn rent. From that rent, the company pays about $121,500 toward the loan, plus the lender's interest. That is close to the price of one robot, every month, and each payment darkens more of the robot.

The robots get older and lose value. In our model the loan shrinks faster, so the gap between the two grows.

After one year, $2,916,700 is still owed and the robots are worth about $5 million. After two years, $1,458,300 and about $3.75 million.

Nothing is owed.

If the payments keep coming, month 36 is the last one. All 50 robots then belong to the company, and nobody else has any claim on them.

In our example they are still worth about $2.7 million, and they keep earning rent.

What if the company fails?

  1. The payments stop. Say it happens at month 18. $2,187,500 is still owed. If nothing had gone wrong the robots would be worth about $4.35 million. In our model a failure cuts that to about $1.95 million if the robots stop, or about $3.05 million if someone keeps them running.
  2. The lender can sell the robots. The loan papers say so from the first day.
  3. The lender is paid first. Anything left goes to the company. If the sale does not cover the loan, the lender takes the loss.

The robot is what is still owed. White dots are paid back even for the arms alone. Blue dots are paid back only if everything installed sells at that share. Dots on the floor are lost either way.

Who does what, and who gets paid.

The company

Runs the robots, pays its 30%, and pays the loan back every month. The owners keep all of the company.

Ferra

Prices the robots, writes up the terms and finds a lender, for a fee of about 1.5%, once. Ferra never lends the money.

The lender

Provides the money and sets its own interest, so we show no rate here. It receives the monthly payments, and it can sell the robots if they stop.

Where the numbers come from

  • Our example is 50 new robot arms, each fully installed with its gripper and controller, at $125,000 each, or $6,250,000 in all. A loan of 70% is $4,375,000, with a fee of about 1.5% ($65,625) when the loan starts, paid back in equal monthly parts over 36 months. For a used robot, our example lends 70% of our estimate of what it is worth, set up and working. Real terms depend on the lender and the robots. Nothing on this site is an offer or commitment to lend.
  • Example values by age follow our model for a robot fully set up and working: about 80% of the new price after one year, 60% after two, 44% after three. See how we work it out.
  • 1,744 public used-robot listings from 7 marketplaces and 26 auction results from 2018 to 2026, collected for Ferra's price tool. Listings show asking prices; auction results show prices paid.

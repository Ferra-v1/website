How a company comes to own its robots.

Robots will do more and more of the world's work, and someone has to own them. Follow one example loan and watch this arm fill as it is paid back. When the arm is full, the company owns every robot.

The company and the loan are an example. Ferra is new and has not arranged a loan yet. The price data is real.

A company asks.

Say a company rents robots out to factories. It wants to buy 50 robot arms, fully installed, at $125,000 each. That is $6,250,000. This arm stands for all 50, and each dot is $1,250 of what they cost.

It asks us how much it could borrow.

We price every robot.

We check what sellers ask for robots like these on 7 marketplaces, and what auctions have really paid.

Then we put a price on each robot, one at a time. These 50 are new, so their price is what they cost.

We would work out the loan.

In our example the loan is 70% of what the robots cost: $4,375,000. The company pays the other 30%, $1,875,000, itself. The dark dots are what the company has paid for.

A rushed sale of the robots would not cover this loan on the first day. See the sums.

We would find a lender.

We would write it all down: the terms, a price for every robot and the study behind our prices.

We would take those papers to lenders, the funds that lend money for machines. Each one reads them, asks questions and says yes or no.

Ferra is new, so this step has not happened yet.

The loan starts.

The lender and the company sign. The lender pays $4,375,000, the robots are bought and they go to work. The light dots are now the lender's money.

The company would pay Ferra's fee, once, on this day: about 1.5% of the loan, or $65,625.

Every month, the company pays.

The robots earn rent. From that rent, the company pays about $121,500 toward the loan, plus the lender's interest. That is close to the price of one robot, every month, and each payment turns more of the arm dark.

The robots get older and lose value. In our model the loan shrinks faster, so the gap between the two grows.

After one year, $2,916,700 is still owed and the robots are worth about $5 million. After two years, $1,458,300 and about $3.75 million.

Nothing is owed.

If the payments keep coming, month 36 is the last one. All 50 robots then belong to the company, and nobody else has any claim on them.

In our example they are still worth about $2.7 million, and they keep earning rent.

Who does what, and who gets paid.

The company

Runs the robots, pays its 30% and pays the loan back every month. The owners keep all of the company.

Ferra

Would price the robots, write up the terms and find a lender, for a fee of about 1.5%, once. Ferra would never lend the money itself.

The lender

Provides the money and sets its own interest, so we show no rate here. It receives the monthly payments, and it can sell the robots if they stop.

What if a loan goes bad?

The lender can sell the robots, and it is paid first. A rushed sale may not cover what is still owed, and then the lender takes the loss.

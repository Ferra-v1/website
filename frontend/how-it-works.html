A company asks.

Say a company rents robots out to factories. It wants to buy 50 robot arms, fully installed, at $125,000 each. The big robot here stands for all 50.

It answers four quick questions on our site: which robots, how many, what they cost, and when it needs the money.

We price every robot.

We check what sellers ask for robots like these and what auctions have paid. Our data holds 1,744 robots listed for sale on 7 marketplaces, and 26 auction results.

Then we put a price on each robot, one at a time. Here, all 50 come to $6,250,000.

We work out the loan.

A loan should be smaller than what the robots could be sold for. That protects the lender if things go wrong.

In our example the loan is 70% of the price: $4,375,000. The company pays the other 30%, $1,875,000, itself. The dark part of the robot is its share.

We find a lender.

We write it all down: the terms, a price for every robot, and the study behind our prices.

We take those papers to lenders, the investment funds that lend money for machines. Each one reads them, asks questions, and says yes or no. Ferra never lends the money itself.

The loan starts.

The lender and the company sign. The lender pays $4,375,000, the robots are bought, and they go to work.

The company pays Ferra's fee, once, on this day: about 1.5% of the loan, or $65,625. It is a loan, so the company still owns all of itself.

Every month, the company pays.

The robots earn rent. From that rent, the company pays about $121,500 toward the loan, plus the lender's interest. That is close to the price of one robot, every month. The dark part of the robot grows with each payment.

The robots get older and lose value. The loan shrinks faster, so the gap between the two keeps growing.

After one year, $2,916,700 is still owed and the robots are worth about $5,003,800. After two years, $1,458,300 and $3,749,600.

The last payment.

Month 36. Nothing is owed. All 50 robots belong to the company, and nobody else has any claim on them.

In our example they are still worth about $2,722,300, and they keep earning rent.

What if the company fails?

  1. The payments stop. Say it happens at month 18. $2,187,500 is still owed, and the robots are worth about $4,352,800 in our model.
  2. The lender can sell the robots. The loan papers say so from the first day.
  3. The lender is paid first. The money from the sale goes to the loan until it is paid.
  4. Anything left goes to the company. If nothing is left and the loan is not covered, the lender takes the loss.

Who does what

The company runs the robots and pays back the loan.

It tells us what it wants to buy, pays its 30%, keeps the robots working, and pays every month.

Ferra prices the robots and arranges the loan.

We price each robot, write up the terms, and find a lender. We never lend the money.

The lender provides the money and is paid back.

It reads the papers, sets the interest, and decides yes or no. It can sell the robots if the payments stop.

What it costs.

Two things: our fee and the lender's interest.

Our fee

About 1.5% of the loan, paid once when the loan starts. In our example that is $65,625.

The lender's interest

This is what the lender charges for the use of its money. Each lender sets its own, so we show no rate here.

What it does not cost

Any part of the company. It is a loan, so the owners keep all of it.

Where the numbers come from

  • Our example is 50 robot arms, each fully installed with its gripper and controller, at $125,000 each, or $6,250,000 in all. A loan of 70% is $4,375,000, with a fee of about 1.5% ($65,625) when the loan starts, paid back in equal monthly parts over 36 months. Real terms depend on the lender and the robots. Nothing on this site is an offer or commitment to lend.
  • Example values by age follow our model for a fully installed robot: about 80% of the new price after one year, 60% after two, 44% after three. Robot arms listed for sale price the arm alone, so they sit lower. See how we work it out.
  • 1,744 public used-robot listings from 7 marketplaces and 26 auction results from 2018 to 2026, collected for Ferra's price tool. Listings show asking prices; auction results show prices paid.
